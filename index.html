The Monument

The Icon of Ancient Rome

The Colosseum — also known as the Flavian Amphitheatre (Anfiteatro Flavio) — stands as one of the greatest engineering achievements of the ancient world. Commissioned by Emperor Vespasian around 72 CE and inaugurated by his son Titus in approximately 80 CE, it remains the largest amphitheatre ever built.

For centuries, this massive structure hosted public spectacles — including gladiatorial contests, animal hunts (venationes), public performances, and reenactments of famous battles. Scholars estimate it could accommodate somewhere between 50,000 and 80,000 spectators across its tiered seating.

Through the Ages

Two Thousand Years of History

From imperial spectacle to eternal monument

⚒️
Construction
c. 72 CE

The Flavian Vision Begins

Emperor Vespasian commissioned the Colosseum on the site of Nero's artificial lake, part of the Domus Aurea complex. Roman engineering — including travertine limestone, tuff, concrete (opus caementicium), and brick — combined in an unprecedented scale of construction.

🏛️
Inauguration
80 CE

Titus Opens the Arena

Emperor Titus inaugurated the Colosseum with approximately 100 days of games. Ancient sources describe elaborate spectacles. Emperor Domitian later added a fourth tier and the underground hypogeum. The structure reached its full monumental form during the Flavian dynasty.

⚔️
The Spectacles
1st–5th c. CE

The Age of Spectacle

For several centuries, the Colosseum hosted gladiatorial contests (munera), animal hunts (venationes), and public executions. Ancient sources suggest that hundreds of thousands of animals were killed over the venue's active period. These events were deeply embedded in Roman political and social life.

🔥
Decline
217–443 CE

Fire and Earthquake

In 217 CE, a severe fire caused significant damage to the upper wooden tiers. Repairs were made, but recurring earthquakes — particularly those of 443 CE and later 1231 CE and 1349 CE — caused major structural damage and the collapse of large sections of the outer south wall.

🏰
Medieval Period
6th–16th c.

Repurposed Through the Ages

After gladiatorial games ended — possibly in the early 5th century — the Colosseum was used as a quarry for building materials, as a Christian shrine, as a fortress, workshops, and even housing at various points. Significant amounts of its original stone were reused in St. Peter's Basilica and other structures.

🌍
Modernity
18th c. – Present

From Ruin to World Heritage

Beginning in the 18th century, popes and scholars began taking preservation seriously. Pope Benedict XIV (1740s) consecrated the Colosseum as a sacred site in memory of Christian martyrs — though modern historians debate the extent of Christian martyrdom there. Today it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of the world's most visited landmarks.

Structure & Engineering

Masterwork of Roman Engineering

An ellipse of stone that redefined human ambition

🏛️

Click the glowing hotspots on the diagram to explore individual architectural elements of the Colosseum.

Structure

🪨
Travertine Limestone

Approximately 100,000 cubic metres of travertine limestone was used in the outer structure, sourced from quarries near Tivoli (Tibur) and transported to Rome by road and river.

🏗️
Elliptical Design

The elliptical shape — approximately 188m × 156m — allowed spectators throughout the arena to see the action clearly while preventing combatants from cornering themselves away from view.

🚪
Vomitoria

An ingenious system of 80 numbered entrances and exit corridors (vomitoria) allowed the Colosseum to be filled or emptied of its tens of thousands of spectators in approximately 15 minutes.

⚓
Roman Concrete

Opus caementicium (Roman concrete), combined with volcanic pozzolana, created extraordinarily durable structures. Modern analysis suggests this concrete may actually strengthen over time through mineralisation.

🌉
The Great Arches

The outer wall features three tiers of 80 arched openings, each decorated with engaged columns: Doric on the first level, Ionic on the second, and Corinthian on the third — a deliberate architectural hierarchy.

⚙️
The Hypogeum

The two-level underground network of passages and chambers — completed under Domitian — used a system of winches, pulleys, and hinged platforms to raise animals and combatants dramatically into the arena.

The Warriors of Rome

Gladiatorial Culture

History, myth, and the reality of the arena

Gladiators were not simply condemned criminals. Many were trained professionals — some even volunteers — who lived and trained in specialised schools (ludi). They occupied a curious social position in Roman society: simultaneously dishonoured and celebrated. Different types of gladiators fought with specific equipment, and certain pairings — such as the Retiarius versus the Secutor — were traditional. Historical evidence suggests that not all combats ended in death; the decision often rested with the organiser (editor) of the games and the crowd.

MURMILLO

Heavy fighter
Equipment Large rectangular shield (scutum), short sword (gladius), visored helmet (galea) with tall crest, arm guard (manica), greave on left leg.

THRAEX

Medium fighter
Equipment Curved short sword (sica), small shield (parmula), griffin-crested visored helmet, arm guard, high leg greaves. Often paired against the Murmillo.
🔱

RETIARIUS

Light fighter
Equipment Large weighted net (rete), trident (tridens), dagger (pugio), shoulder guard (galerus). Minimal armour — no helmet — relying on agility and range. Traditionally paired vs. Secutor.
🛡️

SECUTOR

Heavy fighter
Equipment Large oval shield (scutum), gladius, smooth rounded helmet with small eye openings — designed to resist entanglement in a net. Arm guard, greave. Paired vs. Retiarius.
The Experience

A Day at the Colosseum

What attending a spectacle in ancient Rome may have been like

01
The Arrival

Enter Through Your Arch

Spectators received numbered tesserae (tokens) indicating which of the 80 arched entrances to use. Each entrance led to specific staircases and seating sections. The system allowed tens of thousands to find their seats with remarkable efficiency — an achievement that modern stadium designers still study.

🚪
👑

02
Social Hierarchy

Take Your Place in Society

Where you sat reflected your social status. The lowest tier (podium) was reserved for senators, magistrates, and Vestal Virgins — closest to the action. Higher tiers seated equestrians, ordinary citizens, and foreigners. The uppermost wooden gallery was for women and the poorest citizens. The emperor occupied the imperial box (pulvinar).

03
The Atmosphere

The Crowd Gathers

The atmosphere was described by ancient writers as intoxicating. Vendors sold food, drink, and programmes (libelli munerarii) listing the day's events. The roar of the crowd — the vox populi — was itself a form of political expression. Emperors who provided spectacular games gained public favour; those who did not risked popular discontent.

🎭
⚙️

04
The Mechanisms

The Arena Opens

Beneath the arena floor, the hypogeum hummed with activity. Animals in cages were positioned on lifts. Scenery — trees, rocks, painted backdrops — was assembled. At the appointed moment, trapdoors in the arena floor burst open and creatures, combatants, or elaborate stage sets emerged — a theatrical spectacle as much as a competitive one.

05
The Spectacle

The Games Begin

A full day of games typically began with animal hunts (venationes), followed by executions (noxii), and culminating in gladiatorial combats in the afternoon. The outcome of bouts was not always predetermined, and the crowd's reaction — thumbs gestures, waving of togas — could influence the fate of defeated combatants.

⚔️
Underground Secrets

The Hypogeum

The hidden machinery beneath the arena floor

From Above

The Arena Floor

The oval arena floor measured approximately 83m × 48m. Originally covered in wooden boards topped with sand (the Latin word harena, or arena, means sand), the floor concealed the elaborate mechanical world below. Two main gates controlled the flow of spectacle: the Porta Pompae for triumphant entries and the Porta Libitinaria for the defeated or fallen.

🚪
Porta Pompae

The Gate of Procession — through which gladiators and performers entered the arena in ceremonial procession at the start of games.

⚰️
Porta Libitinaria

Named for Libitina, goddess of funerals — this gate was used to remove the fallen from the arena. Ancient sources suggest workers would dress as Charon (the ferryman of the dead) to collect the bodies.

☀️
Trapdoors

At least 28 trapdoors in the floor connected to the lifts of the hypogeum below, enabling dramatic sudden appearances of animals, scenery, and combatants.

Beneath the Arena

The Underground Network

The hypogeum — added under Emperor Domitian — was a two-level underground structure covering the entire area beneath the arena floor. Two central corridors and twelve side passages created a labyrinthine network. Mechanical lifts (at least 28), operated by teams of workers using counterweights and capstans, could raise animals and platforms through trapdoors directly into the arena.

🔄
Mechanical Lifts

At least 28 lift shafts, operated by counterweights and capstans, could raise platforms of animals, scenery, or performers up to 7 metres into the arena in seconds.

🦁
Animal Holding

Corridors included cages and pens for exotic animals — lions, tigers, bears, crocodiles, elephants — brought from across the Roman Empire for the venationes (animal hunts).

🎭
Staging & Scenery

Entire landscape scenes — including artificial forests, rock formations, and painted backdrops — could be assembled underground and raised into the arena, creating theatrical spectacles rivalling any modern stage production.

Eternal City

The Colosseum in the Heart of Rome

Surrounded by millennia of history

The Colosseum stands at the heart of Rome's archaeological zone, surrounded by some of the most significant monuments of the ancient world. The Roman Forum lies immediately to the north-west, the Palatine Hill to the south, and the Arch of Constantine — the largest surviving Roman triumphal arch — stands just outside its south-east wall.

Did You Know

Fascinating Facts

01
🏆

One of the New Seven Wonders

The Colosseum was voted one of the New Seven Wonders of the World in a global poll conducted in 2007, alongside structures such as the Great Wall of China, Machu Picchu, and the Taj Mahal.

02
⚡

Lightning-Fast Evacuation

The system of 80 numbered vomitoria (exit corridors) was so efficiently designed that scholars estimate the Colosseum could be fully evacuated in approximately 15 minutes — a feat of engineering still admired by modern stadium designers.

03
🌊

Ancient Water Spectacles?

Some ancient sources describe naumachiae — simulated sea battles — being held in the flooded arena. However, modern scholars debate whether the arena was flooded after the hypogeum was completed, or whether these events occurred before that construction.

04
🏗️

Recycled Building Material

During the medieval period, the Colosseum was heavily quarried for building materials. Significant amounts of its stone were reused in the construction of St. Peter's Basilica and other Renaissance-era buildings across Rome. Scholars estimate only about two-thirds of the original structure survives.

05
🌿

A Flora All Its Own

Over the centuries, the Colosseum developed a remarkable microecology. In the 19th century, botanists catalogued over 400 species of plants growing within and around the ruins — including species potentially introduced via seeds carried in animal fodder transported from distant parts of the Roman Empire.

06
🔴

Capital Punishment Symbol

Since 1999, the Colosseum's exterior is lit in gold rather than white whenever a jurisdiction anywhere in the world abolishes capital punishment or grants a condemned prisoner a reprieve. The gesture reflects the Colosseum's transformation from a site of death into a global symbol of life.

Separating History from Legend

Myth vs. Historical Reality

Click each card to reveal the historical truth

Myth

"Every gladiatorial combat ended in the death of the loser."

↩ Click to reveal the reality
Historical Reality

"Many combats ended without a fatality."

Gladiators were expensive to train. Killing them was costly. Ancient evidence suggests many fights ended when one combatant was incapacitated, with the decision to spare or kill resting with the editor (the sponsor of the games) and the crowd. The thumbs gesture (pollice verso) is debated; its exact meaning remains uncertain.

Myth

"The ancient Romans always called it 'the Colosseum.'"

↩ Click to reveal the reality
Historical Reality

"Its ancient name was the Flavian Amphitheatre."

Romans called it the Amphitheatrum Flavium (Flavian Amphitheatre), after the Flavian dynasty that built it. The name 'Colosseum' derives from the medieval Latin 'Colosseum' or 'Coliseum,' likely referencing a colossal statue of Nero that stood nearby — the Colossus of Nero.

Myth

"Large numbers of Christians were martyred specifically in the Colosseum."

↩ Click to reveal the reality
Historical Reality

"The historical evidence is debated and uncertain."

While Christians were certainly persecuted in ancient Rome, direct ancient evidence for mass Christian martyrdom specifically within the Colosseum is limited and contested among modern historians and archaeologists. The association grew strongly in the medieval period. The site was nonetheless consecrated in their memory in the 18th century.

Myth

"Gladiators were slaves who were forced to fight against their will."

↩ Click to reveal the reality
Historical Reality

"Many gladiators were volunteers seeking fame and fortune."

While some gladiators were slaves or prisoners of war, a significant number were free volunteers (auctorati) who signed contracts with gladiatorial schools (ludi) for pay, food, lodging, and medical care. Successful gladiators became celebrities, with their own fan followings and commercial endorsements — not unlike modern athletes.
